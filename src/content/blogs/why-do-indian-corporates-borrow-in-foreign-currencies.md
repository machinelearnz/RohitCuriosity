# Why Do Indian Corporates Borrow in Foreign Currencies?

With persistent waves of External Commercial Borrowings (ECBs) and recurring surges in FCNR(B) deposits, a fundamental corporate finance question warrants closer inspection: Why should an Indian company with predominantly domestic revenues borrow abroad? Is tapping offshore capital a quantifiable arbitrage, or is it an exercise in unhedged currency speculation wrapped in qualitative justifications?

Every corporate treasury team encounters a standard dilemma when evaluating cross-border debt:
*   **The Pitch:** Domestic interest rates are high, making offshore borrowing appear visibly cheaper.
*   **The Risk:** Unhedged USD debt exposes domestic cash flows to severe FX risk; yet fully hedging via swaps often erases the interest rate savings entirely.
*   **The Tactical Compromise:** Corporates either maintain partial hedges—taking on speculative risk—or rely on central bank interventions that temporarily suppress hedging costs to lock in cheaper all-in rates.

To evaluate whether foreign borrowing creates real economic value, we must examine historical currency behavior, corporate risk failures, parity mechanics, and central bank interventions.

## Part 1: The Macro Baseline — USD/INR and Interest Rate Differentials

A ten-year macroeconomic baseline tracking Indian sovereign yields, U.S. sovereign yields, and the USD/INR exchange rate illustrates the mechanical cost of unhedged debt.



![FRED Chart: 10Y Yields and USD/INR Spot Index](/images/img_1788688551919_FRED_-_10_year_chart.png)


*Data Source: Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis (FRED)*

The macroeconomic record highlights three interacting variables:
1.  **India 10-Year Government Bond Yields:** Hovering between 6.5% and 8.0%, representing long-term Rupee interest rates.
2.  **U.S. 10-Year Government Bond Yields:** Dropping to sub-1.0% in 2020 before climbing steadily to 4.2%–4.5% by 2026, setting the baseline for long-term Dollar rates.
3.  **INR per USD Spot Exchange Rate Index:** Moving from a base of 100 in late 2016 to approximately 144 by mid-2026.

This index movement represents a 44% appreciation of the USD against the INR, translating to a ~3.7% compound annualized depreciation of the Rupee.

Historically, if an Indian corporate borrowed in USD unhedged at a nominal rate of 2% to 3%, the "true" realized borrowing cost in Rupee terms matched domestic rates at ~5.7% to 6.7% once factoring in the 3.7% annualized depreciation. However, the current 2026 high-rate environment—with SOFR exceeding 4.5%—pushes the unhedged Rupee-equivalent borrowing cost above 8.0%, making stringent risk management critical.

## Part 2: When "Cheap" Debt Breaks the Balance Sheet



![Unanticipated FX Risks!](/images/img_1788688453328_FX_Risk.png)


*Image Source: Simplilearn*

Corporate balance sheets often suffer when treasury teams mistake structural currency depreciation for temporary volatility, leaving long-term foreign obligations unhedged.

*   **Power Finance Corporation (Q4 FY2020):** Infrastructure financier PFC maintained less than 50% of its foreign currency liabilities hedged. When the COVID-19 shock triggered rapid Rupee depreciation, PFC recorded a staggering ₹5,000 crore foreign exchange loss—wiping out over half of its normalized annual profit and dragging its Tier-I capital down to 12.45%.
*   **The FCCB Crisis (Post-2008):** Prior to 2008, companies like Suzlon Energy and Wockhardt issued zero-hedge Foreign Currency Convertible Bonds (FCCBs), assuming rising stock prices would trigger equity conversion. When the 2008 crash collapsed stock prices and depreciated the Rupee by 25%, bondholders demanded cash redemptions. Forced to buy expensive USD with depreciated Rupees, issuers faced devastating balance sheet shocks, triggering widespread defaults and restructurings.

## Part 3: Theoretical Foundations vs. Empirical Evidence

Interest rate parity theory drives the pricing of forward rates through two core concepts:
*   **Covered Interest Rate Parity (CIP):** Dictates that the forward premium on a currency perfectly offsets the interest rate differential between two countries. If this axiom held perfectly, the fully hedged cost of a USD borrowing would be mathematically identical to borrowing onshore in INR, eliminating any arbitrage.
*   **Uncovered Interest Rate Parity (UIP):** Applies to unhedged exposure, stating that the expected future change in the spot exchange rate should perfectly offset the interest rate differential.

In practice, empirical evidence reveals a structural divergence from these theories. Acharya and Vij (2020) demonstrated that corporate foreign currency borrowing in India is frequently driven by a speculative carry trade motive rather than fully hedged macro arbitrage. Firms essentially bet that actual INR depreciation will be lower than what is implied by forward points or interest rate differentials. Furthermore, Borio et al. (2016) noted that CIP is frequently broken by both structural reasons (dollar liquidity shortages) and non-structural reasons (capital controls and central bank interventions).

---

## Part 4: Recent Events — The 2026 Malhotra Special Swap

Central bank policy can directly engineer CIP breakdowns. In June 2026, under RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra, the RBI introduced a Special Swap Facility that fundamentally restructured the economics of foreign borrowing.

*   **Zero-Cost FCNR(B) Swaps:** The RBI offered commercial banks an at-par USD-INR swap (0.0% hedging cost) on 3-to-5-year foreign currency deposits. Bypassing interbank forward premiums of ~3.5%, this allowed banks to pass a massive 17% to 27% leveraged, tax-free dollar return to non-resident depositors, mobilizing an unprecedented $127.2 billion.
*   **Fixed 1.5% ECB Capped Swap:** For Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) like PFC and REC Limited, the RBI capped the swap hedging cost at a fixed 1.50% per annum for 3-to-5-year tenors.

This policy intervention completely altered the historical corporate borrowing paradigm. Previously, as documented by Acharya and Vij, Indian firms had to run immense unhedged UIP carry trades because the market-quoted forward premium of ~3.5% wiped out nominal interest rate savings. By introducing a subsidized, fixed 1.5% swap rate, the RBI converted a highly risky, unhedged carry trade into a risk-free, fully hedged cost-of-capital arbitrage. The broader systemic question of who bears the cost of subsidizing this FX hedge and managing the resulting ~INR 10 Trillion liquidity surplus remains a complex macroeconomic trade-off.

## Part 5: The Strategic and Qualitative Rationale

Even when forward premia normalize and eliminate direct cost arbitrage, large corporates maintain foreign borrowing programs for long-term strategic capital architecture:

*   **Preserving Domestic Capacity:** Large conglomerates tap international syndicated loans to keep their domestic banking limits entirely free for working capital, localized operational needs, and domestic acquisitions.
*   **Establishing a Global Credit Curve:** Issuing offshore debt forces a company to undergo rigorous international credit ratings and institutional due diligence. This establishes a public pricing benchmark, signals high governance standards, and creates a primed pipeline for future global equity or debt syndications.
*   **Tenor and Volume Mismatch:** The domestic Indian corporate bond market lacks the depth to absorb the massive, ultra-long-tenor debt (10 to 30 years) required for capital-intensive sectors. Global pools of capital provide the duration and sheer check size that the onshore market cannot yet efficiently facilitate.

## In Conclusion

Indian corporates borrow in foreign currency driven heavily by interest rate differentials and carry trade motives, maintaining a bias toward speculative, unhedged exposures when UIP relationships break down. There are instances of 
highly opportunistic, true cost optimizations, motivated by central bank interventions and capital controls -- that push the all-in cost of funds below domestic rates.

In case of mature corporates, with strategic objectives - offshore borrowing evolves from a simple interest rate arbitrage into an essential pillar of corporate capital management.
